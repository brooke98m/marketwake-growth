Ruben — Vision Exercise
What I need most from Ruben to grow
Critical
01
Share the map, not just the destination
One peer described a pattern of "knowledge gatekeeping" — not malicious, but instinctive. Ruben holds the technical decision-making and priority-setting process in his head. The team sees the output but not the reasoning. To grow into a true technical leader, the process of how decisions get made needs to be as visible as the decisions themselves.
What this looks like in practice
Instead of: Choosing a tech stack or framework and assigning tasks.
Try: Running a 15-minute decision walkthrough where the team sees the criteria, tradeoffs, and why this path won. This turns every technical decision into a training moment.
Critical
02
Communicate delays before they become surprises
A peer noted that when timelines slip, Ruben tends to absorb the problem silently and work through it rather than raising it early. This comes from a good place — ownership and responsibility. But the effect is that stakeholders learn about delays late. The difference between "we might be a day behind" on Tuesday and "we're a day behind" on Thursday is enormous for trust.
What this looks like in practice
Instead of: Working late to close a gap and only flagging it if you can't close it.
Try: A simple same-day Slack message: "Heads up — [project] is tracking slightly behind. I have a plan, but wanted visibility." This protects trust without requiring permission.
Growth Edge
03
Move from reactive to proactive communication
One reviewer described Ruben's communication as clear but "usually reactive." Another noted he is mostly proactive but falls short on communicating phase-level development timelines. The pattern: Ruben is excellent when asked, but the next level of leadership means the team and stakeholders hear from him before they have to ask.
What this looks like in practice
Try: A brief weekly status update (even 3 bullets in Slack) covering: what shipped, what's in progress, and what's at risk. This transforms his visibility from "responds well" to "leads the narrative."
Growth Edge
04
Protect scope to protect quality
Both peers and Ruben himself identified a tendency to overcommit. His self-review lists "focus constraint" as a growth area. A peer noted that quality occasionally slips under pressure. The eight-reasons site reveals just how many side projects and unofficial responsibilities he carries. Saying no to one thing is saying yes to doing everything else at a higher level.
What this looks like in practice
Try: Before accepting new work, run a 30-second check: "If I take this on, what gets less attention?" If the answer is "the thing I already committed to," the answer to the new request is "not yet."
Leadership Shift
05
Delegate to develop, not just to offload
Ruben's self-review names delegation as a priority: he wants to delegate more so the team grows while he focuses on high-level planning, QA, and orchestration. This is the right instinct. The shift is from "I delegate when I'm overloaded" to "I delegate because this is how someone on my team gets better." That reframe changes what gets delegated and to whom.
What this looks like in practice
Try: Identify one task per sprint that is slightly above a team member's current level. Give it to them with clear guardrails and a check-in point. This is how you build the bench that lets you operate at the level you described in your vision exercise.
Leadership Shift
06
Increase in-office presence and visibility
A peer directly noted that recent remote work has reduced engagement with the broader team. For someone moving from individual contributor to technical leader, presence is influence. The informal conversations, the hallway questions, the moments where someone overhears you solving a problem — these are how a Development Director becomes a company-wide technical voice, not just a dev team lead.
What this looks like in practice
Try: Anchor 2-3 in-office days per week, especially overlapping with cross-functional meetings and leadership touchpoints. Remote days can be deep-work days for framework and tooling projects.

The path forward — What the next 12 months require
The Invitation
Stop being the best developer in the room. Start being the reason every developer in the room gets better.
Ruben's technical skills are not in question. His peers trust him. His self-awareness is strong. His vision for the dev team — frameworks, automation, compounding infrastructure — is exactly right. The gap is not in what he can build. It is in how visibly he leads.

The career path he described — from task-oriented development to planning, execution, and growth — requires four shifts:

1. Make decisions out loud. Share the reasoning, not just the result. The team learns from how you think, not just what you decide.

2. Communicate before you are asked. Weekly updates. Early timeline flags. Phase-level visibility. The team and stakeholders should never have to wonder where things stand.

3. Delegate for development. Every sprint, one task goes to someone who will stretch to complete it. This is how the team grows and how you earn the orchestration role you described.

4. Protect the 20%. Framework work, tooling, internal infrastructure — these only happen if they are defended against the pull of client delivery. This is the investment that changes what the dev team is capable of in 12 months.

Ruben has earned the trust of his team. Now the work is making his leadership as visible as his craft.
